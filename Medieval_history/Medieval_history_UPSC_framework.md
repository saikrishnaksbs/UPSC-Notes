# 1. Early Medieval India & The Tripartite Powers (Palas, Pratiharas, Rashtrakutas)

### 1. Historical Background
- Post-Harsha north India lacked a paramount unifying political authority.
- Kanauj (Kanyakubja) emerged as the supreme symbol of imperial sovereignty.
- Three major regional powers contested for control over the Gangetic plains.

### 2. Rise to Power
- **Palas**: Gopala founded dynasty in Bengal (c. 750 CE) ending internal anarchy (*Matsyanyaya*).
- **Gurjara-Pratiharas**: Nagabhata I consolidated power in Malwa (c. 730 CE), repulsing Arab incursions.
- **Rashtrakutas**: Dantidurga overthrew the Badami Chalukyas (c. 753 CE), establishing Manyakheta.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Dharmapala expanded Pala power to Kanauj, installing Chakrayudha as tributary ruler.
- Pratihara King Vatsaraja defeated Dharmapala in the Doab region.
- Rashtrakuta King Dhruva defeated both Vatsaraja and Dharmapala, dominating northern India.
- Pratihara Nagabhata II and Mihira Bhoja recaptured Kanauj, establishing imperial hegemony.

### 4. Administration
- Kings assumed supreme titles: *Parama-bhattaraka, Maharajadhiraja, Parameshvara*.
- Empire divided into *Bhuktis* (provinces) governed by *Uparikas*.
- *Bhuktis* divided into *Mandalas* or *Vishayas* under *Vishayapatis*.
- Village administration managed by headman (*Gramapati*) and elder council (*Mahattaras*).
- System dominated by powerful hereditary feudal chiefs (*Samantas* and *Mahasamantas*).

### 5. Revenue System
- *Bhaga*: Sovereign share of agricultural produce, traditionally assessed at one-sixth.
- *Bhoga*: Periodic customary offerings of firewood, fruits, and flowers to king.
- *Hiranya*: Direct agricultural tax collected strictly in cash gold or bullion.
- *Udranga*: Water tax or levy on permanent settled tenant farmers.
- Proliferation of tax-free land grants (*Brahmadeya* and *Agrahara*) to temples and Brahmanas.

### 6. Military System
- Maintained massive standing armies supplemented by feudal levies of tributary *Samantas*.
- Palas maintained formidable naval fleets on the Ganga and massive war-elephant contingents.
- Pratiharas maintained the finest cavalry forces in India, importing Central Asian war-horses.
- Rashtrakutas maintained large standing infantries and hill-fort garrisons in the Deccan.

### 7. Society
- Four Varnas fragmented into hundreds of localized, endogamous occupational sub-castes (*Jatis*).
- Emergence of the **Kayastha** caste as professional administrative scribes and accountants.
- Proliferation of untouchability; *Antyajas* forced to dwell outside village boundaries.
- Condition of women deteriorated; child marriage and *Sati* spread among martial elites.

### 8. Economy
- Agrarian expansion through forest clearance and royal land-grant colonization.
- Relative contraction of long-distance urban commerce and metallic currency in north India.
- Active Bay of Bengal maritime trade from Bengal ports to Southeast Asia (*Suvarnabhumi*).
- Western coastal trade handled by Arab merchants in Rashtrakuta ports (Sanjan, Chaul).

### 9. Religion
- Palas were great imperial patrons of **Mahayana and Vajrayana (Tantric) Buddhism**.
- Pratiharas championed Puranic Vaishnavite and Shaivite devotional temple cults.
- Rashtrakutas maintained broad tolerance, patronizing Shaivism, Vaishnavism, and Jainism.
- King Amoghavarsha I was a devoted patron of Jaina Acharya Jinasena.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- **Pala School of Art**: Graceful black basalt sculptures at Nalanda and Bodh Gaya.
- Somapura Mahavihara (Bangladesh): Monumental terracotta brick Buddhist monastery.
- **Kailasanatha Temple (Cave 16, Ellora)**: Monolithic rock-cut temple carved top-down under Krishna I.
- Elephanta Caves: Colossal three-headed rock-cut **Maheshamurti (Trimurti)** sculpture of Shiva.
- Pratiharas built early Nagara temples at Osian (Surya temple, Harihara temple).

### 11. Literature
- *Kavirajamarga*: First classical Kannada poetic work, authored by Rashtrakuta King Amoghavarsha I.
- *Ramacharitam* by Sandhyakar Nandi: Narrates the Kaivarta peasant rebellion under Palas.
- *Prachandapandava* and *Kavyamimamsa* by Rajashekhara: Celebrated dramatist patronized by Pratiharas.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Sulaiman the Merchant** (851 CE): Praised Rashtrakutas as one of world's four empires.
- **Al-Masudi** (915 CE): Described Pratiharas as *Al-Juzr*, noting their immense cavalry.

### 13. Decline
- Continuous 200-year warfare exhausted royal treasuries and military resources of all three.
- Feudal vassals (*Samantas*) asserted independence as central authority weakened.
- In northwest, Ghaznavid invasions delivered the final death blow to Gurjara-Pratiharas.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Transitioned northern and western India into early medieval decentralized feudal statecraft.
- Spread Indian cultural, Buddhist, and artistic traditions across Tibet and Southeast Asia.
- Architecture reached absolute aesthetic pinnacle in rock-cut monolithic engineering at Ellora.

# 2. The Ghaznavids & Ghurids (Early Turkish Invasions)

### 1. Historical Background
- Rise of militarized Turkish slave dynasties under the Samanid Persian empire.
- Central Asian steppes produced disciplined, mobile mounted cavalry archers.
- Northwestern India was politically fragmented into multiple rival Rajput clan kingdoms.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Sabuktigin established independent Turkish principality at Ghazni (modern Afghanistan).
- Succeeded by son **Mahmud of Ghazni** (998–1030 CE), launching trans-Indus raids.
- Later, the Shansabani dynasty of Ghor overthrew the Ghaznavids under **Muhammad Ghori**.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Mahmud of Ghazni conducted 17 military expeditions into northern India (1000–1027 CE).
- Sacked rich temple metropolises: Nagarkot, Mathura, Kanauj, and Somnath (1025 CE).
- Muhammad Ghori invaded Punjab; captured Multan (1175 CE) and Lahore (1186 CE).
- Routed by Prithviraj Chauhan at First Battle of Tarain (1191 CE).
- Decisively routed Prithviraj Chauhan at **Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE)**.
- Defeated Jaichandra Gahadavala at **Battle of Chandawar (1194 CE)**.

### 4. Administration
- Governed newly conquered Indian territories through trusted military slave-governors (*Mamluks*).
- Qutbuddin Aibak appointed viceroy of Delhi and northern Indian territories.
- Bakhtiyar Khalji assigned eastern campaigns; conquered Bihar and Bengal (c. 1202–1204 CE).
- Established military cantonments (*Thanas*) along major riverine transit highways.

### 5. Revenue System
- Initial phase focused on extracting military plunder, temple treasuries, and war indemnities.
- Subjugated Rajput rajas forced to pay heavy annual monetary tribute (*Kharaj*).
- Imposed **Jizya** on non-Muslim populations under direct Turkish garrison control.

### 6. Military System
- Tactical supremacy based on fast, disciplined, stirrup-equipped **Central Asian horse archers**.
- Used iron horseshoes, improving cavalry endurance across rough stony Indian terrains.
- Indian infantry and slow war-elephants were encircled and destroyed by mobile horse-archers.
- Maintained strict meritocratic military command through elite trained slave-soldiers (*Bandagan*).

### 7. Society
- Capture and deportation of thousands of Indian artisans and laborers to Ghazni.
- Disrupted traditional Rajput martial monopoly; broke upper-caste military dominance.
- Widespread destruction of Buddhist monasteries triggered the terminal collapse of Buddhism.

### 8. Economy
- Massive drainage of accumulated temple gold and silver bullion to Central Asia.
- Introduction of new technologies: The **spinning wheel (Charkha)** and paper-making technology.
- Re-opened direct overland caravan trade routes connecting north India with Islamic heartlands.

### 9. Religion
- Invasions accompanied by iconoclastic zeal, targeting conspicuous wealth of idolatrous shrines.
- Mahmud of Ghazni assumed title **Butshikan** (Idol-Breaker) for domestic prestige.
- Paved the way for permanent settlement of Sufi saints in northern India.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Destruction of Somnath, Thanesar, and Mathura temple architectural complexes.
- Destruction of Nalanda and Vikramashila monastic universities by Bakhtiyar Khalji.
- Beginning of Indo-Islamic structural adaptations using stone spolia from dismantled temples.

### 11. Literature
- **Kitab fi Tahqiq ma li'l-Hind** by **Al-Biruni**: Masterpiece on Indian philosophy, astronomy, and society.
- **Shahnameh** by **Firdausi**: Epic Persian poem celebrating Iranian mythic history at Ghazni.
- **Prithviraj Raso** by **Chand Bardai**: Braj Bhasha court epic eulogizing Prithviraj Chauhan.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Al-Biruni** (1017–1030 CE): Documented Hindu philosophy (*Gita*), mathematics, astronomy, and social customs.
- Noted insular arrogance of Indian scholars: *"Our country is the best, our religion best."*

### 13. Decline
- Mahmud's empire disintegrated due to lack of stable administrative institutionalization.
- Muhammad Ghori was assassinated on Indus banks (1206 CE) by Khokhar rebels.
- Absence of male heirs prompted Ghori's slave-generals to partition his empire.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Shattered traditional multi-state Rajput balance of power in northern India permanently.
- Established permanent Muslim political dominion in northern India, culminating in Delhi Sultanate.
- Facilitated immigration of Persian scholars, Sufi mystics, and Central Asian administrative traditions.

# 3. Delhi Sultanate: Slave Dynasty (Mamluks)

### 1. Historical Background
- Assassination of Muhammad Ghori in 1206 CE created a succession crisis.
- Ghori's Indian territories were governed by his trusted Turkish slave-commander.
- Established the first independent Islamic dynasty seated permanently inside India.

### 2. Rise to Power
- **Qutbuddin Aibak** proclaimed himself Sultan at Lahore in 1206 CE.
- Severed ties with Central Asian Ghazni; laid foundation of Delhi Sultanate.
- Iltutmish (1211–1236 CE) defeated rival slave-generals Yildiz and Qabacha, securing Delhi.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Iltutmish annexed Ranthambore, Mandor, Jalore, Bayana, and Gwalior fortresses.
- Re-asserted Sultanate sovereignty over rebellious governors of Bengal and Bihar.
- Masterful diplomacy: Refused shelter to Jalaluddin Mangbarani, averting Genghis Khan's invasion.
- Balban (1266–1287 CE) pacified Mewat, Doab, and Awadh, securing Delhi's immediate perimeter.

### 4. Administration
- Iltutmish secured Caliphal investiture patent (*Mansur*) from Abbasid Caliph (1229 CE).
- Formed **Turkan-i-Chahalgani (The Forty)**: Elite corporate council of trusted Turkish slave-officers.
- **Raziya Sultan** (1236–1240 CE): First woman ruler of Delhi; broke purdah; promoted non-Turks.
- **Balban's Kingship Theory**: Claimed divine right (*Zil-i-Ilahi* = Shadow of God; *Niyabat-i-Khudai*).
- Balban enforced Persian court rituals: **Sijda** (prostration) and **Paibos** (kissing monarch's feet).
- Created centralized military ministry: **Diwan-i-Arz** headed by *Ariz-i-Mamalik*.
- Established dedicated imperial spy network: **Barids** reporting directly to Sultan.

### 5. Revenue System
- Institutionalized the **Iqta System**: Land revenue assignments granted to officers for troops.
- *Wajh*: Portion of Iqta revenue retained by *Muqti* for personal salary.
- *Fawazil*: Surplus revenue remitted by *Muqtis* to central imperial treasury.
- Taxed agricultural produce through customary *Kharaj*, collected primarily through village headmen.

### 6. Military System
- Maintained standing royal slave-troops (*Khasakhel*) garrisoned in strategic frontier forts.
- Balban reorganized army under *Diwan-i-Arz*; regularized weapon supplies and cavalry muster rolls.
- Constructed network of defensive frontier forts along Beas river to repel Mongols.

### 7. Society
- Rigid Turkish racial aristocracy; indigenous Indian Muslims (*Hindustanis*) excluded from high office.
- Balban refused to appoint low-born individuals, claiming noble descent from *Afrasiyab*.
- Elite military slavery (*Bandagan*) functioned as primary vehicle for socio-political mobility.

### 8. Economy
- Iltutmish introduced pure metallic currency standard: Silver **Tanka** (175 grains) and copper **Jital**.
- Growth of urban craft centers in Delhi, Multan, Lahore, and Kara.
- Influx of artisan refugees from Mongol-ravaged Central Asia enriched urban manufacturing.

### 9. Religion
- Maintained nominal loyalty to the Sunni Abbasid Caliphate of Baghdad.
- Balban balanced orthodox Ulema influence with pragmatic, secular state laws (**Zawabit**).
- Welcomed early Chishti and Suhrawardi Sufi saints into northern Indian towns.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- **Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque (Delhi)**: Built by Aibak (1193 CE) using disassembled temple columns.
- **Qutb Minar**: Initiated by Aibak; completed by Iltutmish; dedicated to saint Bakhtiyar Kaki.
- **Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra (Ajmer)**: Mosque constructed by Aibak with carved stone screens.
- **Sultan Garhi (Delhi, 1231 CE)**: Earliest monumental Islamic tomb built by Iltutmish.
- **Tomb of Balban**: Features the earliest **true masonry arch** in Indian architecture.

### 11. Literature
- **Tabaqat-i-Nasiri** by **Minhaj-us-Siraj**: Detailed contemporary history of early Mamluk rulers.
- **Taj-ul-Ma'asir** by **Hasan Nizami**: First official history of Delhi Sultanate.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- Diplomatic envoys from Abbasid Caliph Al-Mustansir arrived at Delhi court (1229 CE).
- Refugees from Central Asia documented the peace and stability of Iltutmish's Delhi.

### 13. Decline
- Paralyzing factional strife among the Forty Nobles (*Turkan-i-Chahalgani*) after Iltutmish.
- Balban's weak successors (Kaiqubad) succumbed to palace debauchery and factional rebellions.
- Jalaluddin Khalji led the **Khalji Revolution (1290 CE)**, terminating Mamluk dynastic rule.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Transformed Delhi from a regional Rajput outpost into a premier Islamic imperial metropolis.
- Standardized the core administrative institutions (Iqta, Tanka currency, Diwan-i-Arz) of the Sultanate.
- Successfully defended India from catastrophic Mongol invasions during the era of Genghis Khan.

# 4. Delhi Sultanate: Khalji Dynasty

### 1. Historical Background
- Overthrow of the racially exclusive Turkish Mamluk aristocracy in 1290 CE.
- Marked the **Khalji Revolution**, opening state offices to Indian Muslims and non-Turks.
- Mongol invasions reached crisis proportions, threatening the survival of Delhi.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Jalaluddin Khalji founded dynasty (1290 CE); enunciated a benevolent, non-tyrannical monarchy.
- **Alauddin Khalji** assassinated his uncle Jalaluddin at Kara (1296 CE), seizing the throne.
- Distributed plundered gold from Devagiri to win the loyalty of Delhi nobles.

### 3. Political Expansion
- **Northern Campaigns**: Annexed Gujarat (1299), Ranthambore (1301), Chittor (1303), and Malwa (1305 CE).
- Repelled six massive Mongol invasions (1299–1306 CE) through ruthless defensive warfare.
- **Southern Campaigns**: Dispatched slave-general **Malik Kafur (Hazar-Dinari)**:
  - Subdued Yadavas of Devagiri (King Ramachandra).
  - Subdued Kakatiyas of Warangal (King Prataparudra II; surrendered Koh-i-Noor).
  - Subdued Hoysalas of Dvarasamudra (King Vira Ballala III).
  - Sacked Pandyan capital Madurai; established tributary suzerainty down to Rameswaram.

### 4. Administration
- Enforced four royal ordinances to suppress noble rebellions:
  1. Confiscated religious land grants, endowments (*Waqf, Inam*), and private fortunes.
  2. Reorganized state espionage network (**Barids** and undercover **Munhiyas**).
  3. Banned intoxicating liquor and gambling completely in Delhi.
  4. Banned matrimonial alliances and social gatherings among nobles without royal permission.
- Asserted complete independence from religious orthodoxy; proclaimed statecraft was independent of Sharia.
- Assumed exalted imperial title **Sikandar-i-Sani** (Alexander the Second).

### 5. Revenue System
- Instituted direct measurement of cultivated land based on standard unit **Biswa**.
- Fixed agricultural land revenue strictly at **one-half (50%) of gross produce**.
- Levied house tax (**Ghari**) and pasture grazing tax (**Chari**) on cattle.
- Suppressed rural hereditary intermediaries: **Khuts, Muqaddams, and Chaudhuris**.
- Established four separate regulated markets in Delhi with fixed statutory prices (**Shahna-i-Mandi**).

### 6. Military System
- First Delhi Sultan to establish a large, permanent **standing army paid entirely in cash**.
- Introduced **Dagh** (branding of cavalry horses to prevent fraudulent substitution).
- Introduced **Chehra / Huliya** (descriptive facial and physical roll of every enrolled soldier).
- Garrisoned reconstructed frontier fortresses with elite troops and catapults (*Manjaniq*).

### 7. Society
- Barani recorded that rural headmen were reduced to poverty, unable to ride horses.
- Severe punishments enforced; market inspectors sliced flesh from shopkeepers who short-weighted goods.
- Thousands of Mongol prisoners (*Neo-Muslims*) were slaughtered outside Delhi following mutinies.

### 8. Economy
- Market regulation ensured low food grain prices in Delhi despite monsoon famines.
- State established public grain storehouses to feed urban populace during distress.
- Vast wealth extracted from southern temple treasuries enriched the central imperial treasury.

### 9. Religion
- Alauddin separated religious dogma from practical secular statecraft (*Zawabit*).
- Refused to consult Ulema on governance, declaring: *"I issue commands beneficial to state."*
- Maintained respect for Chishti Sufi saint **Hazrat Nizamuddin Auliya**.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- **Alai Darwaza (1311 CE)**: Southern gateway to Quwwat-ul-Islam; finest true arch and dome.
- **Siri Fort**: Second historic city of Delhi, constructed as a defensive military capital.
- **Hauz Khas (Hauz-i-Alai)**: Vast water storage reservoir excavated to supply Delhi.
- **Alai Minar**: Unfinished colossal minaret designed to be twice the height of Qutb.

### 11. Literature
- **Amir Khusrau**: Celebrated court poet; authored *Khazain-ul-Futuh*, *Tughlaqnama*, and *Qiran-us-Sadain*.
- Pioneered early Hindavi poetry; developed classical musical genres of Qawwali and Khayal.
- **Mir Hasan Dehlvi**: Renowned lyric poet; authored *Fawa'id-ul-Fu'ad*.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- Eyewitness accounts of Persian chroniclers documented the siege of Chittor and Ranthambore.
- Amir Khusrau documented southern campaigns of Malik Kafur in *Khazain-ul-Futuh*.

### 13. Decline
- Extreme administrative centralization depended entirely on Alauddin's personal terrifying vigor.
- Malik Kafur's brief despotic regency triggered bloody palace assassinations.
- Last ruler Mubarak Khalji was murdered by Khusrau Khan (1320 CE), ending dynasty.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Successfully broke the military back of the terrifying Mongol menace, preserving Indian civilization.
- First Muslim dynasty to conquer peninsular Deccan and South India, inaugurating southern integration.
- Pioneered advanced military logistics (cash pay, Dagh, Chehra) and centralized market price regulation.

# 5. Delhi Sultanate: Tughlaq Dynasty

### 1. Historical Background
- Khalji dynasty collapsed in bloody factional turmoil following Alauddin's death (1316 CE).
- Ghazi Malik, veteran frontier warden of Dipalpur, marched on Delhi (1320 CE).
- Founded the Tughlaq dynasty, restoring administrative order and orthodox governance.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq defeated usurper Khusrau Khan; crowned Sultan at Delhi (1320 CE).
- Re-annexed Kakatiya kingdom of Warangal under his ambitious son Prince Jauna Khan.
- Succeeded in 1325 CE by Jauna Khan, who ascended as **Muhammad bin Tughlaq (MBT)**.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Muhammad bin Tughlaq achieved the maximum territorial expansion of the Delhi Sultanate.
- Empire comprised **23 imperial provinces (Mukhtas)**, stretching from Peshawar to Madurai.
- Reversed Alauddin's tributary policy; brought southern Deccan under direct provincial administrative rule.

### 4. Administration
- Muhammad bin Tughlaq's Five Controversial Visionary Projects:
  1. *Taxation in Doab (1326 CE)*: Arbitrary tax hike during famine provoked massive rural uprisings.
  2. *Transfer of Capital to Daulatabad (1327 CE)*: Relocated capital to Deccan; disastrous exodus; abandoned.
  3. *Token Currency (1329–1330 CE)*: Bronze coins struck at silver value; rampant domestic forgery; redeemed.
  4. *Khurasan Expedition Plan (1329 CE)*: Mobilized 370,000 troops; disbanded due to shifting Persian alliances.
  5. *Qarachil Expedition (1330 CE)*: Himalayan expedition into Kumaon; decimated by mountain guerrillas and monsoons.
- Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388 CE) reversed centralization; made administration and military **Iqtas hereditary**.
- Established specialized departments: **Diwan-i-Bandagan** (slaves), **Diwan-i-Khairat** (charities), and **Dar-ul-Shifa** (free hospital).

### 5. Revenue System
- Muhammad bin Tughlaq created specialized agriculture ministry: **Diwan-i-Amir-Kohi**.
- Advanced state agricultural relief loans (**Sondhar / Taccavi**) to peasants to dig wells.
- Firoz Shah abolished 24 oppressive un-Islamic cesses (*Abwabs*); retained only 4 Sharia taxes:
  - *Kharaj* (land tax), *Zakat* (alms), *Jizya* (poll tax), *Khams* (spoils of war).
  - Imposed **Jizya on Brahmanas for the first time** in Sultanate history.
  - Levied **Haqq-i-Sharb** (canal irrigation tax assessed at 10% of crop produce).

### 6. Military System
- MBT maintained massive multi-ethnic standing army comprising Turks, Mongols, Afghans, and Indians.
- Firoz Shah compromised military efficiency by paying soldiers with land revenue vouchers (**Itlaq**).
- Made military posts hereditary; allowed elderly soldiers to dispatch sons or sons-in-law to muster.

### 7. Society
- Firoz Shah amassed over **180,000 royal slaves (*Bandagan*)**, employing them in state crafts.
- In later reigns, slave factions dominated court politics, destabilizing the dynasty through assassinations.
- Severe decline in public security; highway robbery proliferated following Muhammad bin Tughlaq's experiments.

### 8. Economy
- Firoz Shah engineered monumental canal networks, boosting agrarian productivity across Haryana and Punjab.
- Founded prosperous new urban cities: **Hissar, Firozpur, Jaunpur, and Firozabad**.
- Maintained 36 royal **Karkhanas** (factories) producing luxury silks, embroideries, and armaments.

### 9. Religion
- Muhammad bin Tughlaq was deeply cosmopolitan; celebrated Hindu festival of **Holi** openly.
- Maintained cordial philosophical dialogues with Jaina scholar **Jina Prabha Suri**.
- Firoz Shah embraced conservative Ulema orthodoxy; banned Muslim women from visiting saintly shrines.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Tughlaq style: Characterized by austere, sloping battered stone walls (*sloping walls*) and rubble masonry.
- **Tughlaqabad Fort (Third City of Delhi)**: Built by Ghiyasuddin with massive cyclopean stone battlements.
- **Tomb of Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq**: Sloping stone tomb with marble dome set inside artificial lake.
- **Firoz Shah Kotla (Fifth City of Delhi)**: Palace-fortress; transported and re-erected two Ashokan pillars.

### 11. Literature
- **Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi** by **Ziauddin Barani**: Classical chronicle evaluating the reigns of MBT and Firoz.
- **Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi** by **Shams-i-Siraj Afif**: Comprehensive historical chronicle of Firoz Shah's administration.
- **Futuhat-i-Firoz Shahi**: Autobiographical memoir authored by Sultan Firoz Shah Tughlaq himself.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Ibn Battuta** (Moroccan, 1333–1342 CE): Served 8 years as Chief Qazi of Delhi under MBT.
- Documented judicial punishments, postal horse system (*Uluq*), token currency failure, and sati.

### 13. Decline
- Territorial overstretch and MBT's erratic projects provoked widespread provincial revolts.
- South India broke away, birthing the independent **Vijayanagara Empire (1336)** and **Bahmani Sultanate (1347)**.
- **Timur's catastrophic invasion (1398 CE)** sacked Delhi, massacred inhabitants, and destroyed Tughlaq power permanently.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Marked the maximum territorial zenith and subsequent irreversible fragmentation of the Delhi Sultanate.
- Pioneered large-scale hydraulic canal engineering in northern India, transforming the agrarian landscape of Haryana.
- Triggered the emergence of powerful independent regional sultanates across the Deccan, Bengal, and Gujarat.

# 6. Delhi Sultanate: Sayyid & Lodi Dynasties

### 1. Historical Background
- Timur's devastating sack of Delhi (1398 CE) left the central Sultanate utterly shattered.
- Northern India splintered into autonomous regional kingdoms (Jaunpur, Malwa, Gujarat, Bengal).
- Delhi became a petty regional principality ruled by localized factions.

### 2. Rise to Power
- **Sayyids (1414–1451 CE)**: Khizr Khan (Timur's appointed governor of Multan) captured Delhi (1414 CE).
- Claimed noble descent from the Prophet Muhammad; ruled nominally as Timurid vassals.
- **Lodis (1451–1526 CE)**: Bahlul Lodi, powerful Afghan governor of Sirhind, deposed last Sayyid ruler (1451 CE).
- Established the **First Afghan Empire** in Indian history.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Bahlul Lodi waged 30-year war against Jaunpur; successfully re-annexed the rich **Sharqi Kingdom (1479 CE)**.
- Sikandar Lodi conquered Bihar, Dholpur, Chanderi, and subdued Gwalior rajas.
- **Sikandar Lodi founded the city of Agra in 1504 CE**, establishing a second imperial capital.

### 4. Administration
- **Afghan Concept of Kingship**: Bahlul Lodi ruled as *primus inter pares* (first among equals).
- Sat on carpets with Afghan tribal sardars; never sat on an elevated formal throne.
- Sikandar Lodi re-asserted central royal dignity, auditing provincial accounts and compelling noble deference.
- Ibrahim Lodi attempted absolute autocracy, imprisoning Afghan nobles and sparking widespread feudal rebellions.

### 5. Revenue System
- Sikandar Lodi introduced the **Gaz-i-Sikandari**: Standardized measuring rod of 32 digits (approx. 30 inches).
- Used as the official land survey standard throughout northern India until Akbar's reign.
- Abolished octroi duties on food grains to alleviate rural economic distress.

### 6. Military System
- Relied on decentralized, tribal Afghan clan levies (Lodhis, Farmulis, Lohanis, Sarwanis).
- Lacked the strict professional discipline and standing cavalry command of the Khaljis.
- Disastrously vulnerable to superior field artillery and mobile flanking tactics of Babur.

### 7. Society
- Afghan tribal egalitarianism conflicted with centralized imperial autocratic governance.
- Assimilation of Afghan settlers into north Indian society, adopting local dialects and dress.

### 8. Economy
- Standardized currency based on the **Bahloli coin**, a mixed copper-silver denomination.
- Recovery of internal riverine commerce following the re-annexation of Jaunpur and the Doab.

### 9. Religion
- Sikandar Lodi displayed severe religious intolerance, destroying Hindu temples at Mathura and Jwalamukhi.
- Re-imposed Jizya; banned Hindu pilgrims from performing ritual ablutions at Yamuna ghats.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Introduced the **Double Dome construction** (inner dome for proportion, outer dome for elevation).
- **Tomb of Sikandar Lodi (Lodi Gardens, Delhi, 1517 CE)**: First specimen of double dome in India.
- **Moth Ki Masjid (Delhi)**: Built by prime minister Miyan Bhoiya; finest specimen of Lodi architecture.
- **Bara Gumbad and Sheesh Gumbad**: Imposing octagonal and square tomb typologies in Delhi.

### 11. Literature
- Sikandar Lodi was a talented poet; composed Persian verses under pen-name **"Gulrukhi"**.
- Commissioned *Farhang-i-Sikandari*: Authoritative medical treatise translating Sanskrit Ayurveda into Persian.
- Commissioned *Lahjat-i-Sikandar Shahi*: Foundational music treatise on Indian classical ragas.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- Afghan merchants and Central Asian horse traders documented the bustling livestock markets of Agra.
- Babur recorded critical, detailed impressions of the disunited Lodi state in his *Baburnama*.

### 13. Decline
- Ibrahim Lodi's high-handed tyranny alienated his own powerful Afghan tribal governors.
- **Daulat Khan Lodi** (governor of Punjab) and uncle Alam Khan invited **Babur** of Kabul to invade India.
- **First Battle of Panipat (April 21, 1526 CE)**: Babur routed Ibrahim Lodi, who died on the battlefield.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Concluded the 320-year history of the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526 CE).
- Introduced the double dome architectural engineering technique that culminated in the Taj Mahal.
- Paved the way for the foundation of the glorious **Mughal Empire** under Babur.

# 7. Vijayanagara Empire

### 1. Historical Background
- Muhammad bin Tughlaq's oppressive misrule triggered deep resentment across the Deccan and South India.
- Two brothers, **Harihara I and Bukka I**, originally served the Kakatiyas of Warangal.
- Captured by Tughlaq, converted to Islam, and dispatched south to suppress rebellions.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Re-converted to Hinduism under the spiritual guidance of Sage **Vidyaranya** of Sringeri.
- Founded the **Vijayanagara Empire in 1336 CE** on the south bank of the Tungabhadra River.
- Established capital at **Hampi (Vijayanagara = City of Victory)**; ruled through four successive dynasties:
  1. *Sangama Dynasty* (1336–1485 CE).
  2. *Saluva Dynasty* (1485–1505 CE).
  3. *Tuluva Dynasty* (1505–1570 CE) — reign of **Krishnadevaraya**.
  4. *Aravidu Dynasty* (1570–1646 CE).

### 3. Political Expansion
- Bukka I annexed the Sultanate of Madurai (celebrated in Gangadevi's *Madhura Vijayam*).
- Devaraya II (1422–1446 CE) expanded dominion across Kerala, Sri Lanka, and coastal Andhra.
- **Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529 CE)** achieved the imperial zenith:
  - Smashed the armies of the Bahmani Sultan Mahmud and captured Raichur Fort (1520 CE).
  - Subdued the Gajapatis of Odisha, capturing Udayagiri and Kondavidu fortresses.
  - Sacked Bijapur and Gulbarga; assumed title **Yavanarajya-Sthapanacharya**.

### 4. Administration
- Monarch assumed sovereign titles: *Raya* and **Hinduraya Suratrana** (Sultan among Hindu Kings).
- **The Nayankara System**:
  - Assigned territorial revenue tracts (*Amara*) to military commanders (**Amaranayakas**).
  - Maintained specified quotas of foot-soldiers, cavalry, and war-elephants for the Emperor.
  - Remitted fixed annual monetary tribute to the central royal treasury.
- **The Ayagar Village System**:
  - Village managed by **12 Hereditary Functionaries (Ayagars)**: Headman (*Gauda*), accountant (*Karnam*), watchman.
  - Maintained with tax-free land plots (*Manya*) and a share of agricultural produce.

### 5. Revenue System
- *Sist*: Standard agricultural land tax, assessed at one-sixth to one-fourth of gross produce.
- Revenue assessed according to soil classification: Wetland, dryland, garden, and forest tracts.
- Levied commercial taxes on professions, markets, transit tolls, and prostitution licenses.
- Maintained stable high-purity gold currency: **Varaha** (also called *Pagoda* or *Hon*).

### 6. Military System
- Maintained massive standing armies, heavily reliant on imported **Arabian war horses**.
- Krishnadevaraya maintained friendly strategic ties with the Portuguese at Goa to monopolize horse supplies.
- Devaraya II recruited skilled Turkish archers to train Vijayanagara cavalry in horse-archery.

### 7. Society
- Women enjoyed high social status: Employed as royal palace bodyguards, wrestlers, astrologers, and accountants.
- Prevalent social evils: Widespread practice of **Sati** and institutionalized **Devadasi** system in temples.
- Caste divisions prominent: *Viprulu* (Brahmans), *Rajulu* (Kshatriyas), *Matikarulu* (merchants), *Nalavajativaru* (laborers).

### 8. Economy
- Celebrated global emporium of international gemstone, diamond, pearl, and textile trade.
- Coastal ports: Bhatkal, Honavar, Mangalore, Calicut, and Masulipatnam handling global sea trade.
- Dominated the lucrative diamond mines of the Deccan plateau.

### 9. Religion
- Early Sangama rulers were Shaivites devoted to Lord **Virupaksha** (form of Shiva).
- Later Tuluva and Aravidu kings embraced Vaishnavism, patronizing **Lord Venkateshwara of Tirupati**.
- Maintained broad tolerance: Constructed mosques for Muslim soldiers and patronized Jaina basadis.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Developed the distinctive **Vijayanagara Architectural Style**:
  - **Raya Gopurams**: Soaring, monumental entrance gateway towers dominating temple complexes.
  - **Kalyana Mandapa**: Elaborately carved open pillared halls for divine wedding ceremonies.
  - **Yali Pillars**: Monolithic pillars sculpted with dynamic rearing mythical beasts ridden by warriors.
  - **Musical Pillars**: Stone pillars producing distinct musical tones when tapped.
- **Vittala Temple (Hampi)**: Famous for its iconic monolithic **Stone Chariot** (Garuda shrine).
- **Hazara Rama Temple**: Walls carved with continuous narrative stone reliefs from the Ramayana.

### 11. Literature
- **Amuktamalyada**: Masterpiece of Telugu poetry authored by Krishnadevaraya on statecraft and religion.
- Patronized the **Ashtadiggajas** (Eight Celebrated Telugu Poets), including **Allasani Peddana** and **Tenali Ramakrishna**.
- Sanskrit masterworks: Krishnadevaraya authored *Jambavati Kalyanam*; Gangadevi authored *Madhura Vijayam*.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Nicolo Conti** (Italian, 1420 CE): Described Hampi's fortifications, currency, and festivals.
- **Abdur Razzaq** (Persian, 1443 CE): Praised Hampi as having **seven concentric defensive walls**.
- **Domingo Paes** (Portuguese, 1520 CE): Eyewitness at Krishnadevaraya's court; compared Hampi to Rome.
- **Fernao Nuniz** (Portuguese, 1535 CE): Recorded detailed administrative histories and court customs.

### 13. Decline
- Regent **Rama Raya** pursued arrogant divide-and-rule diplomacy, playing rival Deccan Sultanates against each other.
- The four Deccan Sultanates formed a grand military alliance against Vijayanagara.
- **Battle of Talikota (Rakshasi-Tangadi, January 1565 CE)**: Sultanate coalition routed Vijayanagara.
- Rama Raya was captured and beheaded; Hampi was systematically plundered, burned, and abandoned.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Served as the formidable southern bulwark of Hindu culture, architecture, and religion for three centuries.
- Telugu, Kannada, and Tamil literatures experienced their greatest classical golden age under royal patronage.
- Hampi ruins stand today as a glorious UNESCO World Heritage monument of medieval urban planning.

# 8. Bahmani Kingdom & The Deccan Sultanates

### 1. Historical Background
- Southern Deccan nobles revolted against the erratic centralizing policies of Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
- Formed an independent Islamic state in the Deccan plateau in 1347 CE.
- Acted as the perennial northern rival of the Vijayanagara Empire for two centuries.

### 2. Rise to Power
- **Alauddin Bahman Shah (Hasan Gangu)** founded the dynasty in 1347 CE; crowned at Gulbarga.
- Claimed descent from mythical Persian hero Bahman; established capital at **Gulbarga (Ahsanabad)**.
- Ahmad Shah Wali shifted the royal capital to **Bidar in 1422 CE** for strategic climatic reasons.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Engaged in relentless wars against Vijayanagara for control of the fertile **Raichur Doab**.
- Firoz Shah Bahmani (1397–1422 CE) defeated Vijayanagara King Devaraya I, marrying his daughter.
- Prime Minister **Mahmud Gawan (1463–1481 CE)** conquered Konkan coast, Goa, and Hubli.

### 4. Administration
- Empire originally divided into 4 large provinces (*Tarafs*) governed by powerful governors (*Tarafdars*).
- **Mahmud Gawan's Administrative Reforms**:
  - Divided the 4 bloated provinces into **8 manageable Tarafs** to curb governor autonomy.
  - Placed one major fort in every province under direct royal crown control.
  - Standardized land revenue measurement and regularized provincial troop muster rolls.
- Permanent court factionalism between **Dakhanis** (resident Muslims) and **Afaqis** (foreign immigrants).

### 5. Revenue System
- Measured arable land and assessed revenue scientifically under Mahmud Gawan's administration.
- Revenue collected through provincial governors; cash salaries paid to regular troops.
- Controlled thriving trade through Arabian Sea ports: **Chaul and Dabol**.

### 6. Military System
- Maintained formidable cavalry contingents and artillery divisions trained by Turkish and Persian mercenaries.
- Deployed gunpowder artillery effectively in Deccan siege warfare against hill fortresses.

### 7. Society
- Bitter ethnic rivalry between native Dakhani Muslims and foreign Afaqi nobles crippled stability.
- Aristocratic African and Ethiopian military commanders (**Habshis**) rose to high commands.

### 8. Economy
- Golconda controlled the world-famous **Kollur Diamond Mines**, yielding the Koh-i-Noor diamond.
- Bidar became globally famous for **Bidriware** (inlaying silver and gold wire into blackened zinc-copper alloy).

### 9. Religion
- Official religion was Islam; early rulers followed Sunni jurisprudence; later Deccan Sultans adopted Shi'ism.
- Patronized prominent Chishti Sufi saint **Hazrat Gesudaraz (Banda Nawaz)** of Gulbarga.
- **Ibrahim Adil Shah II of Bijapur** was titled **"Jagat Guru"** for profound religious tolerance.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- **Mahmud Gawan's Madrasa at Bidar (1472 CE)**: Three-storeyed college in Persian style with glazed tiles.
- **Gol Gumbaz (Bijapur)**: Mausoleum of Muhammad Adil Shah; **second largest dome in the world**.
- Famous for its **Whispering Gallery**, where the faintest whisper echoes eleven times across the dome.
- **Charminar (Hyderabad, 1591 CE)**: Built by Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah to commemorate eradication of plague.
- **Ibrahim Rauza (Bijapur)**: Exquisite mausoleum known as the "Taj Mahal of the Deccan".

### 11. Literature
- Promoted **Dakhani Urdu** as an official literary court language alongside classical Persian.
- Ibrahim Adil Shah II authored **Kitab-i-Navras**: Collection of musical poems praising Saraswati and Ganesh.
- Ferishta composed the celebrated historical chronicle **Tarikh-i-Firishta** at Bijapur court.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Afanasy Nikitin** (Russian merchant, 1469–1472 CE): Documented Bidar, trade, and stark peasant poverty.
- Documented Mahmud Gawan's lavish lifestyle: *"The Grand Vizier is served by 500 dancers daily."*

### 13. Decline
- Court conspiracy framed Mahmud Gawan; Sultan Muhammad Shah III tragically executed him (1481 CE).
- Execution destroyed administrative cohesion; Bahmani state disintegrated into **Five Deccan Sultanates (1518–1527 CE)**:
  1. *Bijapur (Adil Shahi)*.
  2. *Golconda (Qutb Shahi)*.
  3. *Ahmadnagar (Nizam Shahi)*.
  4. *Bidar (Barid Shahi)*.
  5. *Berar (Imad Shahi)*.
- All five sultanates were eventually annexed by the Mughal Empire under Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Introduced Persian architectural engineering and the exquisite Bidriware metallurgy to peninsular India.
- Sired the unique Dakhani Urdu linguistic tradition, bridging northern and southern cultural idioms.
- Gol Gumbaz remains an unmatched acoustic and structural engineering marvel of medieval world architecture.

# 9. The Provincial Kingdoms (Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, Bengal, Kashmir)

### 1. Historical Background
- Tughlaq empire's disintegration (14th century) allowed provincial governors to declare independence.
- Regional dynasties fostered localized vernacular literatures, architectures, and cultural syncretism.

### 2. Rise to Power
- **Gujarat**: Zafar Khan declared independence (1407 CE); established Gujarat Sultanate.
- **Malwa**: Dilawar Khan Ghori declared independence (1401 CE); shifted capital to Mandu.
- **Jaunpur**: Malik Sarwar established the **Sharqi Dynasty (1394 CE)**; "Shiraz of the East".
- **Bengal**: Ilyas Shah united Bengal (1342 CE); founded Ilyas Shahi dynasty.
- **Kashmir**: Shah Mir established first Muslim sultanate in Kashmir (1339 CE).

### 3. Political Expansion
- **Mahmud Begarha of Gujarat (1459–1511 CE)**: Conquered formidable hill forts of **Girnar** and **Champaner**.
- **Hoshang Shah of Malwa**: Expanded borders across Narmada; fortified natural plateau of Mandu.
- **Ibrahim Shah Sharqi of Jaunpur**: Annexed parts of Bihar and Awadh; patronized scholars.
- **Alauddin Husain Shah of Bengal**: Annexed Kamata (Assam), northern Odisha, and parts of Tripura.
- **Zain-ul-Abidin of Kashmir**: Expanded territory into Ladakh, Baltistan, and western Punjab foothills.

### 4. Administration
- Governed through provincial ministers modeled on the Delhi Sultanate Wizarat.
- **Zain-ul-Abidin (Budshah)** of Kashmir established formal price control and judicial registries.
- Employed both Hindu and Muslim administrators based strictly on talent and loyalty.

### 5. Revenue System
- Measured agricultural land and regularized revenue collection in fertile river deltas.
- Gujarat collected massive maritime customs at ports: **Cambay, Surat, Broach, and Diu**.
- Bengal derived wealth from rich wetland paddy yields and flourishing silk monopolies.

### 6. Military System
- Gujarat built a formidable naval fleet to combat Portuguese aggression in the Arabian Sea.
- Bengal maintained extensive riverine flotillas (*Nawwara*) to fight in waterlogged delta tracts.
- Malwa utilized heavy cavalry and mountain fortresses to withstand neighboring Gujarat and Mewar.

### 7. Society
- Rise of regional vernacular identities transcending religious divisions.
- Close social collaboration between Hindu merchant bankers (*Mahajans*) and Muslim provincial rulers.

### 8. Economy
- Gujarat was the premier textile manufacturing and bullion-importing province of medieval India.
- Bengal exported fine muslins, raw silk, and rice across the Indian Ocean networks.

### 9. Religion
- **Zain-ul-Abidin of Kashmir**: Abolished Jizya; banned cow slaughter; restored exiled Hindu pandits.
- **Husain Shahi Bengal**: Fostered syncretic **Satyapir** cult blending Hindu and Muslim folk devotions.
- Gujarat Sultans patronized both Sufi shrines (Sarkhej) and flourishing Jaina mercantile temples.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- **Jaunpur Sharqi Style**: Omitted minarets; used massive sloping entrance pylons (**Atala Masjid**).
- **Mandu Style (Malwa)**: **Jahaz Mahal** (Ship Palace), **Hindola Mahal**, and **Hoshang Shah's marble tomb**.
- **Gujarat Style**: **Sidi Saiyyed Mosque** at Ahmedabad, famous for delicate tree-of-life stone **Jali** screens.
- **Bengal Style**: Brick mosques with curved **Bangla eaves** and terracotta relief plaques (**Adina Masjid**).

### 11. Literature
- **Bengali**: Husain Shah patronized Maladhar Basu (*Sri Krishna Vijaya*); awarded title *Gunaraja Khan*.
- Krittibas Ojha translated Ramayana into Bengali; Kavindra Parameshwar translated Mahabharata into Bengali.
- **Kashmiri**: Zain-ul-Abidin commissioned Persian translations of Mahabharata and Kalhana's *Rajatarangini*.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Duarte Barbosa** (Portuguese, 1516 CE): Described Cambay as one of the world's greatest trading cities.
- **Ludovico di Varthema** (Italian): Recorded the immense wealth and navy of Mahmud Begarha.

### 13. Decline
- Regional dynasties exhausted resources in mutual border wars (e.g., Jaunpur vs. Lodis; Malwa vs. Gujarat).
- Bahlul Lodi conquered and annexed Jaunpur back into Delhi Sultanate (1479 CE).
- All remaining provincial kingdoms (Gujarat, Malwa, Bengal, Kashmir) were annexed by **Akbar**.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Preserved and nurtured classical regional arts, vernacular literatures, and architectural styles.
- Proved that political decentralization could trigger intense cultural creativity and inter-communal syncretism.
- Provided architectural blueprints (e.g., Hoshang Shah's tomb) that directly inspired imperial Mughal monuments.

# 10. The Bhakti Movement

### 1. Historical Background
- Emerged originally in South India (6th–9th c. CE) through Tamil Alvar and Nayanar saints.
- Reacted against dry Vedic ritualism, animal sacrifices, and rigid caste hierarchies.
- Migrated northward in the 14th century, transforming into a pan-Indian socio-religious phenomenon.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Spiritual authority derived from personal, passionate devotion (**Bhakti**) rather than birth status.
- **Ramanuja** (11th c.) formulated philosophical foundation of devotion in **Vishishtadvaita**.
- **Ramananda** (14th c.) bridged South to North, preaching in popular vernacular Hindi.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Bhakti was a grassroots socio-religious movement, not a military or territorial empire.
- Expanded through popular songs, community singing, and congregational gatherings across India.
- Influenced monarchs: Vijayanagara kings, Husain Shah of Bengal, and Akbar respected saints.

### 4. Administration
- Institutionalized egalitarian community spaces:
  - **Sangat**: Congregational assembly where all devotees sat as spiritual equals.
  - **Pangat / Langar**: Free community kitchen where all castes dined together, destroying caste taboos.
  - **Satra & Namghar**: Monastic community networks established by **Sankaradeva** in Assam.

### 5. Revenue System
- Maintained through voluntary personal charity and shared community contributions.
- Rejected state patronage and tax exemptions; saints lived humble domestic working lives.

### 6. Military System
- Early Bhakti was non-violent, preaching spiritual surrender, peace, and universal love.
- Later Sikh tradition militarized under **Guru Hargobind (Miri and Piri)** to resist Mughal persecution.
- **Guru Gobind Singh** established the martial **Khalsa Panth (1699 CE)** to fight tyranny.

### 7. Society
- Boldly attacked the divine justification of caste superiority based on birth.
- Championed equality of all human souls before God (*"Jati pati puchhe nahi koi"*).
- Prominent female saints broke patriarchal shackles: **Mirabai, Andal, Akka Mahadevi, Lal Ded**.

### 8. Economy
- Nirguna saints emerged from working urban craft and peasant classes:
  - *Kabir* (weaver), *Raidasa* (tanner), *Sena* (barber), *Dhanna* (farmer).
- Sanctified manual labor; rejected ascetic renunciation of productive economic work.

### 9. Religion
- Divided into two primary theological branches:
  1. **Nirguna Bhakti**: Devotion to formless, transcendent God (**Kabir, Guru Nanak, Dadu Dayal**).
  2. **Saguna Bhakti**: Devotion to God with human form (**Tulsidas, Surdas, Mirabai, Chaitanya**).
- Rejected temple idol monopoly, animal slaughter, pilgrimage rituals, and scriptural fundamentalism.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Inspired the development of devotional music, classical dances, and temple sculptures.
- **Sankirtana**: Communal singing and ecstatic dancing popularized by Chaitanya Mahaprabhu.
- Temple complexes dedicated to Krishna and Rama expanded across Vrindavan, Ayodhya, and Puri.

### 11. Literature
- Revolutionized Indian literature by adopting popular spoken regional vernaculars:
  - *Hindi / Awadhi*: Tulsidas's **Ramcharitmanas**; Kabir's **Bijak** and *Dohe*.
  - *Braj Bhasha*: Surdas's **Sur Sagar**; Mirabai's devotional *Padas*.
  - *Bengali*: Chaitanya-inspired Vaishnava Padavali literature; *Chaitanya Charitamrita*.
  - *Marathi*: **Abhangas** of Sant Tukaram, Jnaneshwar's *Bhavartha Dipika*, Eknath, Namdev.
  - *Assamese*: **Borgeet** hymns and theatrical plays (*Ankiya Nat*) composed by Sankaradeva.
  - *Punjabi*: Sacred hymns preserved in the **Guru Granth Sahib (Adi Granth)**.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- European travelers (Bernier, Tavernier) recorded the immense popular influence of wandering Bhakti singers.
- Documented mass gatherings at sacred regional pilgrimage shrines (Puri, Pandharpur, Varanasi).

### 13. Decline
- Movement gradually institutionalized into rigid sectarian sub-groups and monastic orders.
- Orthodoxy re-absorbed many communities back into modified caste structures over centuries.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Demolished the exclusive monopoly of Sanskrit, establishing modern Indian regional literatures.
- Bridged communal divides, fostering mutual tolerance and syncretism between Hindus and Muslims.
- Provided the spiritual and cultural foundation for Sikhism and the Maratha national awakening.

# 11. The Sufi Movement

### 1. Historical Background
- Emerged in Persia and Central Asia as an ascetic mystical reform within Islam.
- Reacted against worldly materialism and corrupt court politics of the Umayyad/Abbasid caliphates.
- Al-Hujwiri (*Data Ganj Bakhsh*) introduced systematic Sufi philosophy to India (11th c. Lahore).

### 2. Rise to Power
- **Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti** established the **Chishti Silsila** at Ajmer (1192 CE).
- Sufi masters (**Pirs / Sheikhs**) commanded immense spiritual authority over both Sultans and common folk.
- Established hospices (**Khanqahs**) across northern, western, and peninsular India.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Expanded spiritually through four major orders (**Silsilas**): Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, and Naqshbandi.
- Spread into the Deccan under **Hazrat Gesudaraz (Banda Nawaz)** of Gulbarga.
- Dispatched disciples (*Khalifas*) across provincial towns, establishing regional spiritual seats.

### 4. Administration
- Governed internally through the strict spiritual lineage of the **Silsila** (Chain of Succession).
- Absolute spiritual obedience of the disciple (**Murid**) to the master (**Pir / Murshid**).
- Communal living organized inside the **Khanqah / Jama'at Khana** under rules of humility.

### 5. Revenue System
- Chishtis lived in voluntary poverty (*Faqr*); rejected permanent royal land grants (*Inam/Waqf*).
- Depended entirely on unsolicited charitable donations (**Futuh**) received daily.
- Suhrawardis in Multan accepted state offices (*Shaikh-ul-Islam*) and accumulated private wealth.

### 6. Military System
- Non-military spiritual movement preaching universal peace, self-discipline, and compassion.
- Naqshbandi order actively intervened in royal politics to enforce orthodox Sharia under Mughals.

### 7. Society
- Promoted inter-communal brotherhood; open to all humans irrespective of caste, creed, or gender.
- Maintained a daily free community kitchen (**Langar**) feeding thousands of poor travelers.
- Interacted warmly with Hindu Nathpanthi yogis, exchanging mystical ideas and breath control techniques.

### 8. Economy
- Integrated remote frontiers into agrarian society; cleared forests around new rural shrines.
- Annual death commemorations (**Urs**) stimulated regional trade fairs, markets, and artisanal craft sales.

### 9. Religion
- Core Metaphysical Concepts:
  - **Wahdat-ul-Wujud**: "Unity of Being" (Ibn Arabi); all creation is an emanation of God; championed by Chishtis.
  - **Wahdat-ul-Shuhud**: "Unity of Witness" (Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi); Creator and creation are distinct.
  - **Zikr**: Continuous rhythmic remembrance and chanting of the divine names of God.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- **Dargah Architecture**: Sacred tomb complexes featuring white marble domes, courtyards, and jali screens.
- Celebrated shrines: Dargah of Moinuddin Chishti (Ajmer), Nizamuddin Auliya (Delhi), Salim Chishti (Fatehpur Sikri).
- Inspired Indo-Islamic floral decorative carving and acoustic open-air courtyards.

### 11. Literature
- **Amir Khusrau**: Disciple of Nizamuddin Auliya; authored Persian masnavis and early Hindavi verses.
- Developed the musical genre of **Qawwali** by blending Persian lyrics with Indian ragas.
- **Malfuzat**: Transcribed conversations of saints (*Fawa'id-ul-Fu'ad* of Nizamuddin Auliya).
- **Maktubat**: Epistolary letters written by masters to disciples (*Maktubat-i-Imam Rabbani*).

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Ibn Battuta**: Visited multiple Sufi hospices, documenting their hospitality, austerities, and Langar kitchens.
- European travelers praised the universal devotion shown by all religions at Chishti Dargahs.

### 13. Decline
- Later hereditary custodians (*Sajjadanishins*) commercialized shrines, engaging in court politics.
- Orthodox theological reactions under Aurangzeb curtailed musical assemblies and syncretic practices.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Softened the harshness of military conquest, humanizing Islamic presence in the Indian subcontinent.
- Pioneered early vernacular literatures (Hindavi, Dakhani, Punjabi) and classical Qawwali music.
- Chishti Dargahs remain the greatest enduring living symbols of inter-faith harmony in South Asia.

# 12. Early Mughals: Babur & Humayun

### 1. Historical Background
- Collapse of the fragmented Lodi Sultanate and rise of powerful regional Afghan and Rajput states.
- Zahir-ud-din Muhammad **Babur**, ruler of Kabul, descended from Timur (father) and Genghis Khan (mother).
- Invited by Daulat Khan Lodi and Rana Sanga to unseat the unpopular Sultan Ibrahim Lodi.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Babur captured Lahore and crossed the Indus with 12,000 disciplined mounted cavalry (1526 CE).
- **First Battle of Panipat (April 21, 1526 CE)**:
  - Defeated Ibrahim Lodi through military tactical genius: Field artillery (**Top-o-Tufang**) and flanking maneuvers (**Tulghuma**).
  - Ibrahim Lodi died on battlefield; Babur proclaimed Emperor at Delhi and Agra.

### 3. Political Expansion
- **Battle of Khanwa (1527 CE)**: Decisively routed the massive Rajput confederacy led by **Rana Sanga of Mewar**.
- **Battle of Chanderi (1528 CE)**: Captured Chanderi fortress from Rajput chieftain Medini Rai.
- **Battle of Ghagra (1529 CE)**: Routed allied Afghan forces along the riverbanks of Bihar.
- Succeeded by son **Humayun (1530 CE)**; faced twin threats from Bahadur Shah of Gujarat and Sher Khan Sur.
- Humayun routed at **Battle of Chausa (1539 CE)** and **Battle of Kannauj (1540 CE)**; fled to Persia.
- Humayun recaptured Delhi and Agra in 1555 CE with Persian military assistance from Shah Tahmasp.

### 4. Administration
- Early governance followed Timurid and Mongol steppe traditions; lacked stable institutionalization.
- Humayun divided court administration astrologically into four departments: Earth, Water, Fire, and Air.
- Empire remained fragile military occupation, highly dependent on the personal charisma of the monarch.

### 5. Revenue System
- Distributed plundered treasury gold of Delhi and Agra lavishly to nobles, earning Babur title **Qalandar**.
- Extracted customary land revenues through existing hereditary rural headmen without systematic surveys.

### 6. Military System
- Revolutionary introduction of **matchlocks and wheeled field artillery** into northern Indian warfare.
- Perfected the **Tulghuma** tactic: Concealing fast-circling flanking cavalry on wings to envelop enemy rear.
- Used **Araba** (chained wooden carts) as a protective mobile barricade for frontline cannons.

### 7. Society
- Influx of refined Turko-Persian, Chagatai, and Central Asian noble families into northern India.
- Humayun faced treacherous desertions and court rebellions from his own ambitious brothers (Kamran, Askari, Hindal).

### 8. Economy
- Babur abolished commercial transit octroi duties (*Tamgha*) to encourage trans-Indus caravan trade.
- Economic recovery severely disrupted by endless succession civil wars between Humayun and Afghan chiefs.

### 9. Religion
- Babur declared the Battle of Khanwa a **Jihad** (holy struggle) against Rana Sanga; broke wine cups.
- Avoided systematic persecution; advised son Humayun in his secret testament to protect cow slaughter and respect all faiths.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Babur introduced the symmetrical Persian geometric terrace garden (**Charbagh**) to India.
- Built **Aram Bagh** (Agra), **Kabuli Bagh Mosque** (Panipat), and **Jami Masjid** (Sambhal).
- Humayun laid the foundation of the second city of Delhi, **Dinpanah** (Sanctuary of Faith).

### 11. Literature
- **Baburnama (Tuzuk-i-Baburi)**: Authored by Babur in **Chagatai Turkish**; masterpiece of candid world autobiographical literature.
- Humayun patronized Persian scholars, astrologers, and founded royal library at Dinpanah.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- Persian ambassadors, Central Asian scholars, and Ottoman artillery gunners (**Ustad Ali Quli**) served in Babur's army.

### 13. Decline
- Babur died prematurely at Agra in 1530 CE, leaving an unsettled, financially exhausted empire.
- Humayun's indecisiveness and military blunders allowed **Sher Shah Sur** to completely expel him from India.
- Humayun died accidentally in 1556 CE after falling down the library stairs of Sher Mandal, Delhi.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Laid the permanent foundational cornerstone of the glorious **Mughal Empire** in India.
- Revolutionized Indian warfare forever through the systematic deployment of field artillery and gunpowder.
- Introduced Persian garden landscapes and candid historical autobiographical writing to Indian court culture.

# 13. Sur Empire (Sher Shah Sur)

### 1. Historical Background
- Farid Khan (Sher Shah), an ethnic Afghan of the Sur tribe, was born at Narnaul (Haryana).
- Managed his father Hasan Khan's jagir at Sasaram, gaining practical experience in rural revenue administration.
- Awarded title **"Sher Khan"** by Bahar Khan Lohani of Bihar for slaying a wild tiger single-handed.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Consolidated control over Bihar; defeated the ruler of Bengal at the **Battle of Surajgarh (1534 CE)**.
- Seized the strategic hill fortress of **Chunar** on the Ganga riverbank.
- Routed Mughal Emperor Humayun at **Battle of Chausa (1539 CE)**; assumed royal title **Sher Shah**.
- Smashed Humayun again at **Battle of Kannauj (Bilgram, 1540 CE)**, seizing Delhi and Agra.
- Established the **Second Afghan Empire (1540–1555 CE)**.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Subdued Bengal and reorganized its administration to prevent provincial rebellions (1541 CE).
- Annexed Malwa (1542 CE) and conquered the strategic fortress of **Raisen (1543 CE)**.
- Invaded Marwar; defeated Rajput hero Jaimal and Rathors at the **Battle of Samel (1544 CE)**.
- Famously remarked after Samel: *"For a handful of bajra (millet), I had almost lost the empire of Hindustan."*
- Besieged Kalinjar Fort (Bundelkhand, 1545 CE); died tragically in an accidental gunpowder explosion.

### 4. Administration
- Masterpiece of centralized administrative efficiency; divided empire into **47 Sarkars (Districts)**:
  - *Sarkar Officers*: **Shiqdar-i-Shiqdaran** (Law, Order, Police) and **Munsif-i-Munsifan** (Judicial & Land Revenue).
  - *Pargana Officers*: Divided into Parganas headed by **Shiqdar**, **Munsif**, **Fotadar** (Treasurer), and **Karkuns** (Scribes).
- Maintained direct royal surveillance; routinely transferred administrative officers every two to three years.
- Impartial Justice: Held court daily; declared justice was the highest religious duty of a sovereign.
- Enforced local community responsibility: Village headmen (**Muqaddams**) were hanged if they failed to catch local thieves.

### 5. Revenue System (The Zabt Prototype)
- Surveyed all cultivated agricultural land using the standardized **Jarib** (bamboo rod with iron rings).
- Prepared official schedule of standard crop yield rates called the **Ray**.
- State share fixed strictly at **one-third (33.3%) of average produce**, payable in cash or kind.
- Issued formal land title deeds (**Patta**) and secured written acceptance deeds (**Qabuliyat**) from peasants.
- Levied minor cesses: *Jaribana* (survey fee 2.5%) and *Muhasilana* (collection fee 5%).

### 6. Military System
- Abolished the undisciplined tribal levy system; built a large **centralized standing army paid in cash**.
- Revived Alauddin Khalji's strict checks: **Dagh** (branding of war-horses) and **Chehra** (descriptive troop rolls).
- Maintained 150,000 cavalry, 25,000 infantry, and 5,000 war-elephants stationed in strategic forts.

### 7. Society
- Enforced rigorous public security; travelers and merchants slept safely in roadside rest-houses without fear.
- Abbas Khan Sarwani recorded: *"An old woman with a basket of gold ornaments could travel safely without guards."*

### 8. Economy
- **The Currency Revolution of Sher Shah**:
  - Abolished debased mixed-metal coinage; created the standard tri-metallic currency of India:
  - Introduced pure silver coin: **Rupiya** (178 grains of pure silver; precursor of modern Indian Rupee).
  - Introduced standard copper coin: **Dam** (380 grains; 40 dams equaled 1 Rupiya).
  - Introduced standard gold coin: **Mohur** (169 grains).
- **The Grand Trunk Road & The 1,700 Sarais**:
  - Reconstructed the ancient **Grand Trunk Road (Sadak-i-Azam)** from Sonargaon (Bengal) to Attock (Indus).
  - Built three additional trunk roads linking Agra with Jodhpur, Burhanpur, and Lahore.
  - Constructed over **1,700 Sarais** (inns) at every two *Krohs* (approx. 4 miles) with separate Hindu/Muslim quarters.
  - Sarais operated as royal postal stations (**Dak Chauki**), ensuring rapid intelligence transmission.

### 9. Religion
- Maintained personal orthodoxy but pursued tolerant, secular administrative policies toward non-Muslim subjects.
- Employed talented Hindu generals, most notably **Brahmajit Gaur**, in the highest military commands.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Transitioned architecture from Delhi Sultanate rubble into refined classical Mughal proportions.
- **Tomb of Sher Shah at Sasaram (Bihar)**: Magnificent octagonal red-sandstone mausoleum standing inside an artificial lake.
- **Qila-i-Kuhna Mosque (Purana Qila, Delhi)**: Highlights refined pointed arches, marble inlays, and geometric recessed niches.
- **Rohtas Fort (Jhelum, Pakistan)**: Colossal defensive stone fortress built to check rebellious Gakhar tribes.

### 11. Literature
- Commissioned administrative chronicles; patronized Persian, Arabic, and Hindi scholars.
- Malik Muhammad Jayasi composed the celebrated Awadhi epic poem **Padmavat (1540 CE)** during Sher Shah's reign.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- Central Asian merchants and Portuguese travelers marveled at the speed and security of Sher Shah's highways.

### 13. Decline
- Succeeded by son Islam Shah Sur (1545–1554 CE), who maintained order through severe codification of laws.
- Islam Shah's death triggered violent succession civil wars among incompetent Sur princes.
- Humayun invaded from Kabul, defeated Sikandar Sur at Battle of Sirhind (1555 CE), restoring Mughal rule.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Provided the administrative, agrarian, and monetary blueprint that Emperor **Akbar perfected and built upon**.
- Invented the modern **Rupiya** currency, which remains the national monetary unit of India today.
- Reconstructed the Grand Trunk Road, the economic and military arterial lifeline of northern India for centuries.

# 14. Mughal Empire: Akbar the Great (1556–1605 CE)

### 1. Historical Background
- Humayun died suddenly in 1556 CE, leaving a fragile, contested kingdom.
- Afghan general Hemu captured Delhi and Agra, proclaiming himself *Raja Vikramaditya*.
- 13-year-old Jalaluddin Muhammad Akbar ascended the throne under regent Bairam Khan.

### 2. Rise to Power
- **Second Battle of Panipat (November 1556 CE)**: Bairam Khan defeated and executed Hemu.
- Ended Bairam Khan's regency (1560 CE) and subsequent Petticoat government (1562 CE).
- Assumed direct autocratic personal command of the Mughal imperial apparatus.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Annexed Malwa (1561 CE), Gondwana under Rani Durgavati (1564 CE), and Gujarat (1572–1573 CE).
- Captured formidable Rajput fortresses of **Chittor (1568 CE)** and **Ranthambore (1569 CE)**.
- Annexed Bengal and Bihar (1576 CE) following the defeat of Daud Khan Karrani.
- **Battle of Haldighati (1576 CE)**: Raja Man Singh defeated Maharana Pratap of Mewar.
- Conquered northern and western frontiers: Kashmir (1586), Sindh (1591), Baluchistan, and Kandahar (1595 CE).
- Southern Deccan campaigns annexed Khandesh, Berar, and parts of Ahmadnagar (1601 CE).

### 4. Administration
- Institutionalized the **Mansabdari System (1571 CE)**:
  - Universal administrative-military framework grading all civil and military officers into a single hierarchy.
  - Dual rank structure: **Zat** (determined personal status and salary) and **Sawar** (cavalry troop quota).
- Imperial territories organized into **15 Subahs (Provinces)** governed by a *Subahdar*.
- Provincial checks and balances: Provincial *Diwan* (Finance) was appointed independently by central *Wazir*.
- Districts (*Sarkars*) managed by *Faujdar* (Executive/Police) and *Amalguzar* (Revenue Collector).
- Cities managed by **Kotwal** (Chief of Police, market superintendent, and municipal registrar).

### 5. Revenue System
- Promulgated the **Ain-i-Dahsala / Zabt System (1580 CE)** designed by **Raja Todar Mal**.
- Measured arable land using the **Tanab** (bamboo rod linked with iron rings).
- Calculated 10-year rolling average of crop yields and prices; state share was **one-third (33.3%)**.
- Classified agricultural land into four soil categories:
  1. *Polaj*: Cultivated continuously every single year.
  2. *Parauti*: Left fallow temporarily for a year or two.
  3. *Chachar*: Left uncultivated for three to four years.
  4. *Banjar*: Wasteland left uncultivated for five or more continuous years.

### 6. Military System
- Enforced strict military muster inspections: **Dagh** (horse branding) and **Chehra** (descriptive troop rolls).
- Maintained diverse military arms: Cavalry, infantry (*Dakhili*), artillery, war-elephants, and river fleets.
- Promoted talented Rajput warrior clans into supreme imperial military commands (*Raja Man Singh*).

### 7. Society
- Rajput diplomatic alliances: Married princesses of Amber, Bikaner, and Jaisalmer without forced conversions.
- Prohibited forcible **Sati** if against the widow's free will; legalized widow remarriage.
- Discouraged child marriage; fixed minimum marriage ages at 16 for boys and 14 for girls.

### 8. Economy
- Standardized currency based on pure silver **Rupiya** and copper **Dam**.
- Agricultural expansion supported by state loans and irrigation encouragements.
- Thriving textile exports of cotton, calicos, silk, and indigo to Europe and West Asia.

### 9. Religion
- Abolished discriminatory taxes: **Pilgrimage Tax (1563 CE)** and **Jizya (1564 CE)**.
- Established **Ibadat Khana (1575 CE)** at Fatehpur Sikri for inter-faith philosophical debates.
- Issued **Mahzar Decree (1579 CE)**: Proclaimed Akbar as **Imam-i-Adil** (Supreme Arbiter) in ambiguous disputes.
- Promulgated **Tauhid-i-Ilahi (Din-i-Ilahi, 1582 CE)**: Ethical spiritual fellowship based on **Sulh-i-Kul (Universal Peace)**.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Blended Persian, Central Asian, and indigenous Hindu stone architectural traditions.
- **Humayun's Tomb (Delhi)**: First classical Mughal garden-tomb with double dome.
- **Fatehpur Sikri**: Masterpiece capital complex: **Buland Darwaza**, Panch Mahal, Diwan-i-Khas, Salim Chishti Tomb.
- Constructed massive strategic red sandstone fortresses at Agra, Lahore, and Prayagraj (Allahabad).

### 11. Literature
- **Akbarnama & Ain-i-Akbari** by **Abul Fazl**: Monumental court history and administrative gazetteer.
- **Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh** by **Abdul Qadir Badauni**: Critical contemporary orthodox history.
- Established the **Maktab Khana** (Translation Bureau): Translated Mahabharata (*Razmnama*), Ramayana, and Rajatarangini into Persian.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Ralph Fitch** (English, 1583–1591 CE): Described Agra and Fatehpur Sikri as far larger than contemporary London.
- Jesuit missionaries (Father Anthony Monserrate and Rodolfo Acquaviva) documented Ibadat Khana debates.

### 13. Decline
- Succession disputes cast a shadow over late reign; Prince Salim (Jahangir) rebelled (1600–1602 CE).
- Salim had Akbar's chief minister and biographer **Abul Fazl assassinated** by Bir Singh Bundela.
- Akbar passed away at Agra in October 1605 CE; buried at Sikandra.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Consolidated the Mughal Empire into a formidable, unified, pan-Indian political and economic powerhouse.
- Formulated the secular, inclusive state policy of **Sulh-i-Kul**, providing enduring ideological stability.
- Created the Mansabdari and Dahsala systems, the administrative backbone of India for over a century.

# 15. Mughal Empire: Jahangir & Shah Jahan (1605–1658 CE)

### 1. Historical Background
- Peaceful succession of Prince Salim as **Jahangir** (1605 CE), inheriting a stable, affluent empire.
- Succeeded in 1628 CE by Prince Khurram as **Shah Jahan**, inaugurating imperial architectural zenith.
- Marked the high noon of Mughal court luxury, fine arts, and international commercial diplomacy.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Jahangir crushed the rebellion of his eldest son Prince Khusrau (1606 CE).
- Shah Jahan seized the throne in 1628 CE, eliminating rival dynastic claimants with Asaf Khan's support.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Jahangir concluded the **Treaty of Mewar (1615 CE)** with Rana Amar Singh, ending 50-year conflict.
- Conquered the impregnable mountain fortress of **Kangra (1620 CE)**.
- Safavid Persian Empire recaptured strategic border fortress of **Kandahar (1622 CE)** from Jahangir.
- Shah Jahan annexed **Ahmadnagar Sultanate (1636 CE)**; forced Bijapur and Golconda to accept vassal treaties.
- Launched expensive, failed Central Asian military expeditions to Balkh and Badakhshan (1646–1647 CE).

### 4. Administration
- Jahangir erected the famous **Chain of Justice (Zanjir-i-Adl)** at Agra fort for direct public petitions.
- Introduced **Du-aspa Sih-aspa** rank in Mansabdari: Doubled cavalry quotas without increasing personal Zat salary.
- **The Nur Jahan Junta (1611–1627 CE)**:
  - Empress Nur Jahan, father Itimad-ud-Daula, and brother Asaf Khan dominated court appointments.
  - Coins were struck bearing Nur Jahan's name alongside the Emperor.
- Shah Jahan maintained a highly centralized bureaucracy managed by talented Prime Ministers (Sadullah Khan).

### 5. Revenue System
- Agricultural revenues reached historic peak of 220 million rupees annually under Shah Jahan.
- Lavish state expenditures on monumental palaces, gardens, and imperial display strained finances.
- Gujarat and Deccan suffered a catastrophic, devastating famine (1630–1632 CE); state organized soup kitchens.

### 6. Military System
- Expanded artillery and cavalry forces; failed repeatedly in three sieges to recover Kandahar (1649–1653 CE).
- Increased induction of Deccani nobles and Maratha sardars (Shahji Bhonsle) into Mughal military service.

### 7. Society
- Period of supreme court luxury, fine muslin fabrics, perfumery, and refined cosmopolitan aristocratic etiquette.
- European traveler Bernier observed stark economic disparities between fabulously rich nobles and impoverished peasantry.

### 8. Economy
- Surat flourished as the premier maritime emporium of the Mughal Empire (**Bandar Mubarak**).
- European East India companies (English, Dutch) established thriving coastal factories for textile exports.
- **The Peacock Throne (Takht-i-Taus)**: Encrusted with millions in precious gems and the **Koh-i-Noor** diamond.

### 9. Religion
- Jahangir executed fifth Sikh Guru **Arjan Dev (1606 CE)** for alleged treason, alienating Sikh community.
- Shah Jahan adopted slightly more orthodox policies, destroying newly built Hindu temples in Varanasi.
- **Prince Dara Shikoh** championed profound syncretism, translating 50 Upanishads into Persian (**Sirr-i-Akbar**).

### 10. Art & Architecture
- **Painting Zenith under Jahangir**:
  - Shifted toward refined naturalism, psychological portraits, and European-inspired halos.
  - Master nature artist: **Ustad Mansur** (painted Siberian crane, dodo); master portraitist: **Abul Hasan**.
- **Architecture Zenith under Shah Jahan**:
  - Replaced red sandstone with glowing **White Makrana Marble** and delicate **Pietra Dura** gem inlays.
  - **Taj Mahal (Agra, 1631–1648 CE)**: Mausoleum for Mumtaz Mahal; chief architect **Ustad Ahmad Lahori**.
  - **Shahjahanabad (Delhi, 1638 CE)**: Built the **Red Fort (Lal Qila)** and the colossal **Jama Masjid**.
  - **Tomb of Itimad-ud-Daula (Agra)**: Built by Nur Jahan; exquisite marble jewel-box architecture.

### 11. Literature
- **Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri**: Brilliant, candid autobiographical memoirs penned by Jahangir himself.
- **Padshahnama** by **Abdul Hamid Lahori**: Official court chronicle of Shah Jahan's glorious reign.
- **Majma-ul-Bahrain** by **Dara Shikoh**: Philosophical treatise proving unity of Hinduism and Islam.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Captain William Hawkins** (1608 CE) & **Sir Thomas Roe** (1615–1618 CE): Visited Jahangir's imperial court.
- **Francois Bernier** & **Jean-Baptiste Tavernier**: Visited Shah Jahan's court; described Peacock Throne and diamond mines.
- **Niccolao Manucci**: Italian traveler and artilleryman serving Prince Dara Shikoh.

### 13. Decline
- Shah Jahan fell severely ill in September 1657 CE, sparking the **War of Succession**.
- Aurangzeb defeated Dara Shikoh at **Battle of Samugarh (1658 CE)** and **Battle of Deorai (1659 CE)**.
- Aurangzeb imprisoned his aging father Shah Jahan inside Agra Fort, seizing the throne.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Marked the cultural and artistic golden age of Mughal civilization, producing world-renowned architectural wonders.
- Jahangiri miniature painting reached the undisputed aesthetic zenith of South Asian visual art.
- The tragic defeat of liberal Dara Shikoh altered the course of Indian history toward religious polarization.

# 16. Mughal Empire: Aurangzeb & Imperial Decline (1658–1707 CE)

### 1. Historical Background
- Aurangzeb Alamgir triumphed in the bloody War of Succession, executing brothers Dara, Shuja, and Murad.
- Inherited a sprawling, wealthy empire facing mounting agrarian strains and regional assertiveness.
- Ruled for 49 years (1658–1707 CE), completely transforming the ideological direction of the state.

### 2. Rise to Power
- Crowned emperor at Delhi in July 1658 CE; adopted imperial title **Alamgir (Conqueror of the World)**.
- Consolidated authority through strict personal puritanism, earning the popular moniker **"Zinda Pir"** (Living Saint).

### 3. Political Expansion
- Extended northeastern frontiers: Mir Jumla conquered **Assam (1662 CE)**; Shaista Khan captured Chittagong (1666 CE).
- **The 25-Year Deccan Campaign (1681–1707 CE)**:
  - Personally shifted entire imperial court and army to Deccan to suppress Marathas.
  - Annexed **Sultanate of Bijapur (1686 CE)** and **Sultanate of Golconda (1687 CE)**.
  - Captured and brutally executed Maratha king **Chhatrapati Sambhaji (1689 CE)**.
- Territorial extent reached all-time maximum: Spanned 21 Subahs from Kabul to Jinji.

### 4. Administration
- Enforced strict Islamic orthodoxy: Banned court music, royal astrology, **Jharokha Darshan**, and **Tula-Dan**.
- Discontinued stamping Islamic Kalma on coins to prevent desecration; banned Persian festival **Nauroz**.
- Appointed **Muhtasibs** (Censors of Public Morals) in major cities to suppress alcohol, gambling, and vice.
- **The Jagirdari Crisis**: Shortage of crown lands (*Be-Jagiri*) to assign to bloated influx of Deccani nobles.

### 5. Revenue System
- Increased imperial revenue demands to 50% or more to finance endless Deccan military campaigns.
- Frequent transfer of Jagirdars led to ruthless, short-sighted over-exploitation of peasant cultivators.
- **The Agrarian Crisis (Irfan Habib Thesis)**: Crushing land revenue sparked widespread armed peasant uprisings.

### 6. Military System
- Maintained the largest standing military force in Mughal history, encamped in the Deccan.
- Incapable of countering swift, mobile guerrilla tactics (**Ganimi Kava**) of Maratha hill cavalry.
- Influx of Maratha and Deccani officers increased Hindu share in Mansabdari to historic peak (**33%**).

### 7. Society & Rebellions
- Series of powerful peasant and regional rebellions:
  - **Jat Peasant Rebellion (Mathura)**: Led by Gokula, Rajaram, and Churaman; desecrated Akbar's tomb.
  - **Satnami Rebellion (1672 CE)**: Armed revolt of lower-caste religious peasants at Narnaul.
  - **Rajput Rebellion (1679 CE)**: Mewar and Marwar revolted over Aurangzeb's interference in Rathor succession.
  - **Sikh Transformation**: Guru Gobind Singh created martial **Khalsa Panth (1699 CE)** against Mughal tyranny.

### 8. Economy
- 25 years of continuous Deccan warfare completely drained the massive imperial treasuries of Shah Jahan.
- Administrative neglect in northern provinces disrupted trade routes, river transport, and agriculture.

### 9. Religion
- Completely reversed Akbar's policy of *Sulh-i-Kul*; ruled strictly according to orthodox Hanafi Sharia.
- Ordered destruction of prominent Hindu temples (Kashi Vishwanath at Varanasi, Keshavdeva at Mathura).
- Executed ninth Sikh Guru **Tegh Bahadur (1675 CE)** for defending Kashmiri Pandits.
- **Re-imposed Jizya (1679 CE)** after 115 years of abolition, alienating millions of Hindu subjects.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Dispersed court painters and musicians; artistic patronage declined sharply at Delhi.
- **Bibi Ka Maqbara (Aurangabad, 1660 CE)**: Built for Queen Dilras Banu Begum; known as "Poor Man's Taj".
- **Badshahi Mosque (Lahore, 1673 CE)**: Monumental red-sandstone mosque with white marble domes.
- **Moti Masjid (Delhi Red Fort)**: Small white marble private prayer mosque for Aurangzeb.

### 11. Literature
- **Fatawa-i-Alamgiri**: Monumental encyclopedic compendium of Islamic jurisprudence compiled by royal scholars.
- **Muntakhab-ul-Lubab** by **Khafi Khan**: Candid, clandestine history recording peasant distress and Maratha wars.
- **Ma'asir-i-Alamgiri** by **Saqi Mustaid Khan**: Detailed official posthumous biography of Aurangzeb.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- **Niccolao Manucci**: Documented Aurangzeb's court intrigues, Deccan wars, and religious measures in *Storia do Mogor*.
- English and French East India Company factors documented Mughal naval skirmishes (Child's War 1686–1690 CE).

### 13. Decline
- The **"Deccan Ulcer"** ruined Aurangzeb physically, militarily, and financially:
  > *"Just as the Spanish ulcer ruined Napoleon, the Deccan ulcer ruined Aurangzeb."*
- Died exhausted at Ahmadnagar in March 1707 CE at age 88; buried in a simple open-air grave at Khuldabad.
- Imperial authority disintegrated rapidly under weak successors, triggering 18th-century fragmentation.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Represented the ultimate territorial zenith and simultaneous structural exhaustion of the Mughal Empire.
- Religious polarization and alienation of Rajputs, Sikhs, and Marathas shattered the imperial social fabric.
- Paved the way for the rise of the **Maratha Confederacy** and eventual British colonial conquest of India.

# 17. The Maratha Empire & The Peshwas (1627–1761 CE)

### 1. Historical Background
- Rugged topography of the Western Ghats (Sahyadris) fostered fiercely independent martial peasant communities.
- Bhakti saints (Jnaneshwar, Namdev, Eknath, Tukaram, Ramdas) created a shared democratic Marathi cultural identity.
- Maratha chieftains served as seasoned military cavalry commanders under the Deccan Sultanates.

### 2. Rise to Power
- **Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj (1627–1680 CE)**: Born at Shivneri Fort to Shahji Bhonsle and Jijabai.
- Captured Torna Fort at age 16; built an independent kingdom (**Hindavi Swarajya**).
- Slew Bijapuri general **Afzal Khan** using tiger claws (*Bagh Nakh*) at Pratapgad (1659 CE).
- Conducted daring midnight raid on Mughal viceroy **Shaista Khan** inside Pune (1663 CE).
- Sacked rich Mughal port city of **Surat (1664 CE)**, humiliating imperial authority.
- Signed **Treaty of Purandar (1665 CE)** with Raja Jai Singh; surrendered 23 forts; visited Agra court (1666 CE).
- Staged daring escape from Agra inside sweet baskets; recaptured all lost fortresses.
- Grand coronation as **Chhatrapati at Raigad Fort (1674 CE)**; officiated by Gaga Bhatta of Varanasi.

### 3. Political Expansion
- Shivaji conducted southern campaigns (1677–1678 CE), capturing Jinji and Vellore in Tamil Nadu.
- Succeeded by Chhatrapati Sambhaji (1680–1689 CE), who fought Mughals relentlessly until captured and martyred.
- **Maratha War of Independence (1689–1707 CE)**: Led by Rajaram, Santaji Ghorpade, Dhanaji Jadhav, and **Queen Tarabai**.
- **The Golden Age of the Peshwas (1713–1761 CE)**:
  - *Balaji Vishwanath (1713–1720 CE)*: Secured Mughal sanad for Chauth and Sardeshmukhi across six Deccan provinces.
  - *Peshwa Baji Rao I (1720–1740 CE)*: Military genius; proclaimed **Hindu-Pad-Padshahi**; expanded Marathas to Delhi.
  - Famously declared: *"Let us strike at the trunk of the withered tree, and the branches will fall of themselves."*
  - Defeated Nizam at **Battle of Palkhed (1728 CE)**; conquered Malwa, Gujarat, and Bundelkhand.
  - *Peshwa Balaji Baji Rao (Nana Saheb, 1740–1761 CE)*: Expanded empire from Attock (Indus) to Cuttack.

### 4. Administration
- Shivaji's Council of Eight Ministers: **Ashta Pradhan** (purely advisory; non-hereditary):
  1. *Peshwa (Mukhya Pradhan)*: Prime Minister; general administration.
  2. *Amatya (Majumdar)*: Finance Minister; audited accounts.
  3. *Sachiv (Shurnavis)*: Royal correspondence; edicts.
  4. *Mantri (Waqia-Navis)*: Royal household intelligence.
  5. *Senapati (Sari-Naubat)*: Commander-in-Chief of army.
  6. *Sumant (Dabir)*: Foreign Minister.
  7. *Nyayadhish*: Chief Justice.
  8. *Panditrao*: Religious affairs and charities.
- Under later Peshwas, administration decentralized into a **Maratha Confederacy**:
  - *Gaekwads of Baroda, Scindias of Gwalior, Holkars of Indore, Bhonsles of Nagpur, Peshwas of Pune*.

### 5. Revenue System
- Measured land directly using the **Kathi** measuring rod; eliminated corrupt zamindari intermediaries.
- Fixed land revenue at **40% of produce** after abolishing all traditional illegal cesses.
- **Chauth**: Military protection tax amounting to **one-fourth (25%) of standard land revenue** from foreign territories.
- **Sardeshmukhi**: Additional hereditary levy of **one-tenth (10%)** claimed by Shivaji as supreme head of Maharashtra.

### 6. Military System
- Mastered **Ganimi Kava** (guerrilla hit-and-run mountain warfare utilizing steep Sahyadri terrain).
- Dual Cavalry Force:
  - *Bargirs*: Regular state-equipped cavalry troopers mounted on royal horses.
  - *Silahdars*: Feudal noble cavalry supplying their own horses and armaments.
- Maintained formidable defensive hill forts and an independent naval fleet commanded by **Kanhoji Angre**.

### 7. Society
- Egalitarian social mobilization: United diverse Marathi castes (Marathas, Kunbis, Brahmans, Mahars).
- Relied on mountain hill-tribesmen (**Mavalas**) as elite infantry and fortress guardians.

### 8. Economy
- Extracted vast annual Chauth and Sardeshmukhi tributes from across Mughal India.
- Pune, Satara, and Nagpur developed into flourishing banking and artisanal trade metropolises.

### 9. Religion
- Proclaimed **Hindavi Swarajya** and protection of cows, Brahmanas, and sacred shrines.
- Maintained profound tolerance: Respected Quran, protected Muslim women, and employed Muslim naval captains.
- Spiritual inspiration drawn from Bhakti saints **Sant Tukaram** and **Samarth Ramdas**.

### 10. Art & Architecture
- Superb mountain military engineering: Over 300 hill forts with double walls and cisterns carved into living rock.
- Sea fortresses: **Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg, and Suvarnadurg**.
- Promoted traditional wooden residential palace architecture (**Wadas**) in Pune.

### 11. Literature
- Promoted Marathi language and **Modi script** for official administrative documents and revenue registers.
- Commissioned *Rajyavyavahara Kosha*: Specialized glossary replacing Persian terms with Sanskrit administrative equivalents.

### 12. Foreign Travellers
- English factory factors at Surat and Bombay documented Shivaji's daring raids and military brilliance.
- Portuguese records at Goa documented Maratha naval supremacy under Kanhoji Angre.

### 13. Decline
- Maratha Confederacy suffered internal factional rivalries among Scindias, Holkars, and Peshwas.
- Over-extended into northern India, alienating potential allies: Rajputs, Jats, and Awadh.
- **Third Battle of Panipat (January 14, 1761 CE)**:
  - Afghan invader **Ahmad Shah Abdali** crushed the Marathas commanded by Sadashivrao Bhau.
  - Decimated 28,000 elite Maratha warriors, shattering dreams of a pan-Indian Maratha empire.

### 14. Historical Significance
- Successfully shattered the Mughal imperial hegemony, becoming the foremost political power in 18th-century India.
- Created the finest indigenous guerrilla military tradition and naval coastal defense in early modern Asia.
- The power vacuum left by Panipat (1761 CE) facilitated the rapid territorial expansion of the British East India Company.

# 18. Indo-Islamic Art & Architecture

## 🏛️ Chronological Evolution of Indo-Islamic Architecture

```text
Architectural Phases of Medieval India:
  ├── 1. Mamluk / Imperial Delhi Style (1193 – 1290 CE): Quwwat-ul-Islam, Qutb Minar, Balban's tomb (first true arch)
  ├── 2. Khalji Style (1290 – 1320 CE): Alai Darwaza (true dome, horseshoe arch), Siri Fort
  ├── 3. Tughlaq Style (1320 – 1414 CE): Sloping battered walls, grey sandstone, austere rubble masonry
  ├── 4. Sayyid & Lodi Style (1414 – 1526 CE): Introduction of double dome, octagonal and square tombs
  ├── 5. Provincial Styles (14th – 16th Century CE): Sharqi (Jaunpur), Malwa (Mandu), Gujarat, Bengal, Deccan
  └── 6. Mughal Imperial Style (1526 – 1707 CE): Red sandstone, white marble, Charbagh layout, Pietra dura, bulbous domes
```

---

## 🔍 Master Architectural Elements Matrix

- **The Arcuate System**:
  - Replaced the indigenous trabeate (beam-and-lintel) technique with the true **arch and keystone**.
  - Replaced flat stone horizontal roofs with the soaring **true dome**.
- **Mortar Technology**:
  - Use of high-grade lime-surkhi hydraulic mortar enabled massive spans across open interior halls.
- **Decorative Motifs (Absence of Human Figurines)**:
  - Strict adherence to Islamic aniconism; avoided sculpting human and animal idols in religious structures.
  - **Calligraphy**: Elegant Quranic verses incised in Naskh, Kufic, and Nastaliq scripts.
  - **Arabesque**: Continuous, flowing mathematical geometric interlacing of stems, leaves, and tendrils.
  - **Geometric Tessellations**: Hexagons, octagons, and star patterns symbolizing infinite cosmic order.
  - **Jali Screens**: Intricate, pierced stone lattice windows filtering blinding tropical sunlight.
  - **Pietra Dura (Parchin Kari)**: Inlaying polished semi-precious gems (lapis lazuli, carnelian, jasper, jade) into marble.

---

## 🕌 Mughal Architecture Epochs & Landmarks

- **Babur & Humayun**:
  - Babur laid out early geometric terrace gardens (**Charbagh**) at Aram Bagh (Agra) and Kabuli Bagh (Panipat).
  - Humayun built the citadel palace of **Dinpanah** in Delhi.
- **Akbar's Age of Red Sandstone**:
  - **Humayun's Tomb (Delhi)**: Designed by Mirak Mirza Ghiyas; first Mughal Charbagh garden-tomb with double dome.
  - **Fatehpur Sikri City (1571–1585 CE)**: Masterpiece complex: Buland Darwaza, Panch Mahal, Jodha Bai's Palace, Diwan-i-Khas.
  - **Agra Fort & Lahore Fort**: Massive red sandstone defensive palace-fortresses with sandstone brackets.
- **Jahangir's Transitional Phase**:
  - **Tomb of Itimad-ud-Daula (Agra, 1622–1628 CE)**: Built by Nur Jahan; first monument built entirely of white marble with **Pietra Dura**.
  - **Shalimar and Nishat Gardens (Kashmir)**: Terraced formal Mughal water gardens with cascading fountains.
- **Shah Jahan's Golden Age of White Marble**:
  - **Taj Mahal (Agra, 1631–1648 CE)**: Chief architect **Ustad Ahmad Lahori**; pinnacle of Mughal architectural symmetry and grace.
  - **Red Fort of Delhi (1638–1648 CE)**: Red sandstone palace-fortress containing Diwan-i-Aam, Diwan-i-Khas, Rang Mahal, and Nahr-i-Bihisht.
  - **Jama Masjid (Delhi, 1656 CE)**: Colossal congregational mosque featuring three white marble domes and two 130-foot minarets.

# 19. Medieval Paintings & Music

## 🎨 Master Schools of Medieval Painting

### 1. The Mughal Imperial Miniature Painting School
- **Origins under Humayun**:
  - Humayun brought master Persian painters **Mir Sayyid Ali** and **Abdus Samad** from Safavid Persia to Kabul and Delhi.
- **Akbar's Royal Painting Studio (Tasvir Khana)**:
  - Established formal imperial atelier managed by Abdus Samad; integrated dozens of talented indigenous Hindu painters.
  - Celebrated Indian painters: **Daswant, Basawan, Kesu, Lal, Mukund, and Tara**.
  - Major Illustrated Manuscripts:
    - *Hamzanama* (Story of Amir Hamza): Massive project spanning 1,400 large paintings on cotton fabric.
    - *Tutinama* (Tales of a Parrot): Early fusion of Persian and Indian folk styles.
    - *Razmnama*: Elaborately illustrated Persian translation of the Hindu epic Mahabharata.
- **Jahangir's Golden Zenith (Naturalism & Portraiture)**:
  - Painting shifted from crowded group narrative compositions to refined, individualized portraiture and nature studies.
  - Master nature and bird artist: **Ustad Mansur** (awarded title *Nadir-ul-Asr*); painted flora, fauna, and rare birds.
  - Master human portraitist: **Abul Hasan** (awarded title *Nadir-uz-Zaman*); painted Jahangir's coronation scene.
  - Master psychological portraitist: **Bishandas**; dispatched to Persia to paint Shah Abbas I.
  - Introduction of the **Halo (Divine Nimbus)** around the head of emperors, inspired by European Christian art.
- **Decline under Aurangzeb**:
  - Court painters dispersed to regional provincial kingdoms: Rajasthan (Mewar, Marwar, Bundi, Kishangarh) and Pahari hills (Kangra, Basohli).

---

## 🎵 Medieval Music & Performing Traditions

- **Amir Khusrau (1253–1325 CE)**:
  - Titled **Tuti-i-Hind (Parrot of India)**; revolutionary pioneer of North Indian classical music.
  - Created the musical genre of **Qawwali** by synthesizing Persian lyric poetry with Indian classical melodies.
  - Credited with introducing musical styles **Khayal, Tarana, and Qalb**.
  - Credited with inventing the stringed **Sitar** (adapting the Veena) and the **Tabla** (adapting the Pakhawaj).
- **The Dhrupad Tradition & Miyan Tansen**:
  - **Gwalior Court of Raja Man Singh Tomar**: Center of the ancient classical **Dhrupad** vocal tradition; compiled the music treatise *Man Kautuhal*.
  - **Miyan Tansen**:
    - Foremost musician at Akbar's imperial court; awarded royal title **Mian** by Akbar.
    - Disciple of the great Vaishnavite saint-musician **Swami Haridas** of Vrindavan.
    - Created celebrated new ragas: **Miyan ki Todi, Miyan ki Malhar, Miyan ki Sarang, and Darbari Kanada**.
- **Royal Music Treatises**:
  - *Gita Govinda* by **Jayadeva** (12th c. Bengal): Celebrated lyrical Sanskrit songs describing love of Radha and Krishna.
  - *Sangita Ratnakara* by **Sarangadeva** (13th c. Yadava court): Authoritative classical musicological text of medieval India.
  - *Tuhfat-ul-Hind* by **Mirza Khan** (reign of Aurangzeb): Encyclopedic Persian work analyzing Indian music theory, grammar, and poetics.
